Choosing a Dentist

How to Tell Whether Your Dentist Is a Good Fit

You found a dentist, but are they the right one for you? Here are the signs to look for, and the warning signs that deserve a second look.

From the Dentistry & Implants team

Check the Credentials

Start with the basics. A dentist should hold a valid state license and have graduated from an accredited dental school. You can verify a license with the state. Beyond that, look at ongoing training in the treatments you need. Dentistry changes, and dentists who keep studying tend to bring better methods into their practice.

At our practice, Dr. GiTae Kwon, DDS MSc, earned his DDS at NYU College of Dentistry and a master's degree in Dental Implantology and Advanced Surgical Extractions, and he teaches implant surgery to other dentists. Read about his background on his doctor page.

Does Your Dentist Listen and Explain?

This matters more than anything else. A good dentist asks about your concerns and your history before recommending anything. They show you what they see in your mouth, explain it in plain words and describe your options, including the simplest one. You should leave understanding what is wrong and what your choices are.

If you feel talked at, hurried or confused, that is worth noticing.

Do They Respect Your Time and Budget?

Appointments should start close to the scheduled time, and the office should be organized about booking and follow-up. On money, a good dentist is open about costs, checks your insurance, gives you an estimate in writing and offers payment options when needed. You should not find out the price after the work is done.

Does the Office Feel Right?

A clean, calm office with a friendly team says a lot. So does how the staff get along. Ask how instruments are sterilized if you are curious. A good office is glad to tell you. Modern equipment, such as digital X-rays and 3D scans, helps with accuracy and comfort, but it matters most when the dentist uses it to explain things to you.

Follow-Up and Prevention

A good dentist cares about what happens after you leave. They remind you about cleanings, check on you after bigger procedures, give you a plan for home care and screen for problems such as gum disease and oral cancer at regular exams. The goal is to keep problems from starting, not just to fix them.

Red Flags

  • Treatment is recommended without a clear explanation or any imaging.
  • You feel pushed to start the most expensive option right away.
  • Many treatments are suggested at once, none of them urgent, and no one explains which can wait.
  • No one can give you a written estimate.
  • No follow-up after a procedure, or no plan for your care.
  • A dirty or chaotic office, or a team that seems dismissive.

One of these does not mean your dentist is bad. A pattern does. And you can always ask for a second opinion, which a confident dentist will not take personally.

Trust Your Experience

Reviews and referrals help, but your own visits are the strongest evidence. Over time you should feel heard, informed and in control of your choices. If you do, you have likely found a good fit.
